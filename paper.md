# A Frozen Sketch Catalogue, Audited: Persistence of Hand-Drawn Scar Identifications in the Blue Spring Manatee Aggregation, 2013–2026

## Abstract

Photo-identification of Florida manatees (*Trichechus manatus latirostris*) is
maintained in the Manatee Individual Photo-identification System (MIPS), which
ingests photographs. Alongside it, the long-running Blue Spring study maintained a
parallel, hand-drawn "Practical Scar Sheet" catalogue — pen-and-ink sketches of
each animal's scar pattern — which was frozen in April 2013 and never audited.
Because the formal archive does not ingest sketches, this catalogue is the kind of
stranded record that disappears without ever being tested. We transcribed the
complete frozen set (210 entries, 209 with catalogue IDs), pre-registered six
predictions — including the sheet's own 3–5-year and 10-year "drop rules" treated
as falsifiable claims about re-identifiability — and joined the transcription to
every public post-2013 record we could source (a published genealogy of the
aggregation and five years of published site updates). The public record reaches
only 21.5% of the catalogue, so the pre-registered fallback applies: we report a
coverage audit and a non-representative case series. Within the joined subset
(n = 45), 34 animals were publicly re-identified after 2013 with a median last
record in 2025, twelve years past the freeze; known deaths account for 24.4%;
scar load did not predict re-identification (HIGH 69.7% vs LOW 66.7%), and the
pre-1990 cohort persisted at least as well as the 2000–2013 cohort (86.7% vs
82.4%) — the reverse of the registered prediction. Two individuals first
identified in the 1970s were still being recognised in 2026. The drop rules are
thus far too pessimistic for every animal the public record can see, and untested
for the silent majority; resolving the latter requires the MIPS join, which we
have formally prepared. The study demonstrates a general recovery method for
identification catalogues stranded in non-archival media: freeze, transcribe,
pre-register, and audit against whatever public ruler exists — reporting coverage
failure as a result, not an embarrassment.

**Keywords:** Florida manatee; photo-identification; scar catalogue; Blue Spring;
data rescue; pre-registration; MIPS

## 1. Introduction

Individual identification by natural markings underpins manatee demography. The
 range-wide system, MIPS, grew from the scar catalogues built by the USGS Sirenia
Project and partners and is now managed by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Research
Institute (FWRI); it is a photographic archive, accessible to researchers. The
Blue Spring aggregation on the St. Johns River has been studied since the 1970s,
and its working identification tool for much of that period was not the
photographic file but a set of hand-drawn scar sheets maintained by Wayne
Hartley: one sketch per animal, annotated with its catalogue (BS) number, name,
year code, size, and the marks by which it is known. A printed "Practical Scar
Sheet" set — the field subset of currently recognised animals — was frozen in
April 2013.

Sketches sit outside every archival pipeline: MIPS ingests photographs, libraries
ingest publications, and a working sketch set is neither. Such records are
routinely lost when a study changes hands, and their scientific content — decades
of accumulated identification knowledge — is lost with them. Yet a frozen sketch
catalogue has one property a living database lacks: it is a fixed, dated claim
about which animals were identifiable, and by what marks, at a known moment.
Joined to later records, it becomes a testable instrument. In particular, the
Blue Spring sheet states its own retention rules on its key page: animals are
dropped from the practical sheet after roughly 3–5 unrecorded years, and
recognised animals after 10. Those rules encode an expert's implicit model of
how long a scar identity survives. No published work has audited them: the
photographic system's accuracy has been assessed (e.g. Langtimm et al. 1998;
Reid et al. 1991), but the sketch catalogue itself — its coverage, its drop
rules, its decade-scale persistence — has never been tested against later
records.

This note performs that audit under a pre-registration fixed before any
transcription or join, and reports the outcome including its central negative
result: the public record is too thin to test most of the catalogue, and that
thinness — quantified — is itself the finding that motivates a formal data
request to the MIPS custodians.

## 2. Methods

**The frozen set.** The complete April-2013 Practical Scar Sheet set (22 pages:
one key page, 21 catalogue pages) plus a 2016 companion adoptee sheet set were
obtained as PDFs from the site warden's published distribution. The catalogue is
alphabetical; each entry is a dorsal sketch with marks drawn in, the BS number
handwritten at lower right, name and year code beneath (year codes give first
identification year and origin, e.g. 70 = Blue Spring 1970; B89 = born 1989).

**Transcription.** Each page was read visually in two independent passes (the
PDF text layer is negligible), recording per entry: name, BS ID, size class,
sex where marked, year code as written and decoded, scar count by the sheet's
own vocabulary (white scars, healed grey scars, cuts, tail section-missing;
freeze brands excluded), a section-missing flag, flipper damage, and a
confidence flag. Disagreements were adjudicated by a third read of the contested
sketches under stated conventions; the full error structure is reported in the
project's transcription methods note. Scar-load groups were fixed from the sheet
alone, before any join: HIGH = section-missing or ≥3 marks; LOW = exactly 1 mark
and no section-missing; MID = the remainder.

**The public ruler.** Every post-2013 public record for a named Blue Spring
individual was extracted from the sources named in the pre-registration:
Hartley's published *Genealogies of the Blue Spring Manatees* (newest edition)
and Save the Manatee Club published updates and articles (2025–2026), each row
carrying its source. Events recorded: resightings, calves born to identified
mothers (a dated proof of the mother's identification), deaths, and last-seen
statements.

**Join and scoring.** The join key is BS ID where a public source states it
(the companion adoptee sheets confirm 17 joins by number) and otherwise the
individual's name within Hartley's own naming system (names are unique in the
set; two spelling variants across sources — Lilly/Lily, Phillip/Philip — are
documented and were resolved explicitly). Six predictions (P1–P6) with fixed
thresholds, a persistence-curve definition, and a fallback rule — if the ruler
joined <40% of the set, results would be reported as a coverage audit and
non-representative case series — were committed to the project repository
before transcription began. Censoring is handled as registered: an animal
absent from public records is counted as *unjoined*, never as dead or
unrecognisable.

## 3. Results

**Coverage.** The ruler joined 45 of 209 ID-bearing entries (21.5%); the
fallback rule therefore governs all reporting. The joined subset is visibly
selected: 33 HIGH, 9 MID, 3 LOW (catalogue: 151/39/20), dominated by genealogy
matriarchs and programme adoptees.

**Scorecard.** P1 (≥60% joinable) failed at 21.5%. P2 (median last-record year
≥2019 among the re-identified) passed: the median is 2025 (n = 34). P3 (HIGH
exceeds LOW re-identification by ≥20 points) failed: 69.7% vs 66.7% (+3.0
points), with MID at 100% (9/9) — no scar-load gradient. P4 (≥15% of joined
with a known death) passed at 24.4% (11/45; deaths 2014–2025). P5 (pre-1990
cohort ≥15 points below the 2000–2013 cohort) failed in the opposite
direction: 86.7% vs 82.4%. P6 (gap-and-return in year-by-year histories) was
void under its registered condition: only five joined animals have ≥4 distinct
presence years in public sources, below the required 30.

**Persistence.** Among joined animals, the share with a public record in or
after year *t* declines from 75.6% (2014) to 44.4% (2025); treating known
deaths as exits rather than identification failures, from 91.1% to 60.0%.
Time from the 2013 freeze to last public record (n = 34) is bimodal: an
early-exit bloc (≤4 years, n = 7) and a large bloc still recorded in 2025–26
(12–13 years, n = 20); median 12 years. Span from first identification to last
record has median 28 years and maximum 56 (an animal first identified in 1970
and recorded in January 2026).

**Worked examples.** (i) BS4 ("Lilly"/"Lily", first identified 1974, 7 marks):
recorded in the 2024, 2025 and 2026 winter aggregations. (ii) BS19 ("Merlin",
1970, 5 marks): recorded December 2024 and January 2026. (iii) BS189 ("Ann",
1996, 5 marks): calves recorded 2014, 2017, 2021, 2023. (iv) BS286 ("Amber",
born 2000, a brand-identified MID animal): calved 2015, died February 2020.
(v) BS260 ("Calista", 1999, 4 marks): no post-2013 resighting; found dead in
2014 — one of seven joined animals whose only post-2013 outcome is a death.

## 4. Discussion

Three conclusions survive the coverage failure. First, **the medium mismatch is
real and consequential**: a complete, expert identification catalogue existed
for one of the best-studied manatee aggregations in the world, in a medium no
archive ingests, and a fifth of it could still be joined to the living record
thirteen years after it froze — by hand, from public sources, in days. The
remaining four-fifths is not shown to be lost; it is shown to be *unverifiable
from outside the researcher-only archive*, which is a different and more
fixable problem.

Second, **the drop rules are visibility rules, not identity rules**, and the
distinction matters for any programme that retires catalogue entries. For every
animal the public record can see, identities persisted far beyond both the
3–5-year practical drop and the 10-year recognised-animal drop — including
animals whose sketches show a single mark, and a MID group (several of them
brand-identified adoptees) that persisted at 100%. Scar load, the intuitive
predictor of re-identifiability, showed no gradient at all (P3), and cohort
age showed the reverse of the expected one (P5) — in a subset, admittedly,
selected for site fidelity. Whether the rules are also too pessimistic for the
silent majority is precisely what the MIPS join would decide, and the audit is
designed so that join can be dropped in without changing any definition.

Third, **coverage failure is a reportable result**. The pre-registered fallback
converted a disappointing join rate from a temptation (quietly analyse the
convenient subset as if it were the population) into the paper's spine: 21.5%
coverage, with the selection mechanism named (genealogies cover matriarchies;
updates cover adoptees), is information about the public record of a protected
species that did not exist before this audit.

## 5. Limitations

One site, one freeze date, one observer's naming system. The public ruler is
not a sighting census: genealogy presence years mark notable events, not
seasons present, so all persistence figures are floors and P6 could not be
run. Transcription of hand-drawn marks carries a measured ±1 count error and a
convention-dependent section-missing rate (11–23%); conclusions are stated
only at the HIGH/MID/LOW granularity those errors support. Nothing here
estimates survival, and no absence in this study is evidence of death or of
identification failure.
